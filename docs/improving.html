Improving the Reconstruction

Everything below is addressed to the next capture, not to the code. The pipeline has been pushed about as far as this footage allows, and most of what limits it now was decided when the cameras were bolted on and the record button was pressed. The useful question is no longer "what else can the solver do" but "what should the rig look like next time".

Each recommendation is tied to a number this project actually measured, so the cost of the current arrangement is visible rather than asserted.

What it costs to have no overlap

The three cameras share no field of view. Cam1 and cam2 point roughly opposite ways at ground level and cam3 looks straight down, so a cabbage seen from the side and the same cabbage seen from above have no common surface. That single fact is responsible for most of the pipeline's complexity and all of its remaining error.

Because nothing can be triangulated between cameras, registration has to come from the robot's motion instead — the hand-eye problem, solved in rig_solve.py. It works, and the physical checks confirm it: the two ground cameras' recovered optical axes come out 175° apart, which is to say the solver worked out they face opposite ways without ever being told the rig's layout. But it is weakly conditioned, and the weakness shows up exactly where it hurts:

The first two rows are the whole story. The transform places a ground camera's view of a plant within about one plant's width of where the nadir camera puts it — but the plants are spaced closer than that, so matching a cam1 plant to a cam3 plant picks the neighbour about as often as the plant. Of 190 plants in the fused field, only a handful are confidently seen by two cameras. The per-plant fusion that was the entire point of having three cameras does not survive its own error bars, and no amount of further solving fixes it, because the information was never captured.

1. Give the cameras overlapping views

This is the single highest-value change and it costs nothing but a bracket. Angle the two ground cameras forward and inward, perhaps 30–45° off their current axes, so that each one's field of view clips the strip of bed the nadir camera is looking at. A shared strip only needs to be a fraction of each frame.

With overlap, a plant seen from the side and from above becomes one observation of one object, and registration stops being an inference from motion and becomes a direct measurement. The residual that currently sits at 0.11–0.14 would be set by feature-matching accuracy, which is pixel-scale — one to two orders of magnitude better, and comfortably below the plant spacing that currently blocks correspondence. The entire 838-line hand-eye solve becomes optional.

Keeping one camera nadir is still right: it is the only view that sees the field as a field, and it is what makes the row structure legible. The change is to stop having the other two look away from it.

2. Add a stereo pair

Everything this pipeline produces is unitless. Monocular reconstruction fixes shape but not size, so each camera's scale is arbitrary and only the ratio between cameras is recoverable — and even that drifts, because monocular scale executes a slow random walk along the trajectory. The measured disagreement between hand-eye's scale and a direct trajectory fit was 7% on cam1.

A stereo pair with a known baseline fixes this at the source. Depth becomes metric per frame, with no drift to accumulate and no scale to solve for. Three specific problems disappear:

  • Unitless output. The SCALE constant in lift_boxes.py exists only because nothing in the footage records real size. Stereo makes plant dimensions millimetres directly.
  • The parked-camera trap. Motion-based depth needs motion. A stationary camera produces a reconstruction that looks cleaner than a moving one, because the network falls back on its monocular prior — it is not triangulated and cannot be stitched, which is why stationary stretches are currently discarded rather than used. Stereo works perfectly well parked.
  • Scale drift between cameras. Stops being a quantity that has to be estimated at all.

For 640×480 sensors working at roughly half a metre to a metre from the canopy, a baseline around 8–12 cm is the usual trade: wide enough for depth resolution at working distance, narrow enough that the two views still overlap heavily on a close, self-similar subject.

3. Trigger the cameras in hardware

The three clips were started by hand. The evidence is in the frame counts — cabbage is 4578 / 4571 / 4561, broccoli 4963 / 4962 / 4953 — with cam3 always shortest and cam1 always longest, which is what three webcams started and stopped by a person look like.

Recovering that offset took an entire script. clip_sync.py correlates per-frame optical-flow magnitude across cameras that share no imagery, because the robot's stop-and-go burst pattern is the only signal common to all three. It works to about a frame, which is not good enough to fuse geometry on, so rig_solve.py then refines the offset as a continuous parameter. On the real pairs that refinement improved the fit by 0–1%: the offset is weakly identified, and the pipeline ends up trusting the correlation estimate anyway.

A hardware trigger, or even a common timecode, deletes clip_sync.py outright and removes a free parameter from the rig solve. This is the cheapest item on the list by engineering effort and it removes a whole class of error.

4. Calibrate the rig once, with a target

Ten minutes with a checkerboard would replace a large fraction of this codebase. Intrinsics per camera and extrinsics between them, measured directly, would make redundant:

  • the hand-eye solve in rig_solve.py, including its conditioning gates, its bootstrap branch-flip diagnostics, and its time-offset refinement;
  • the soil-plane tilt correction in fuse.py, which exists only because hand-eye's two tilt components ride on a near-singular (I − RA) at one degree of rotation and cannot be trusted;
  • the trajectory-alignment scale fit, for the same reason.

All three are inferences standing in for a measurement nobody took. They are reasonable inferences — the self-test recovers a known transform to 1.5° and 0.4% on scale — but a calibration target beats them and never has a bad day.

5. Put a known length in the frame

Short of stereo, a single real measurement converts the whole model to real units: the nadir camera's height above the soil, or a fiducial of known size left in shot at the start of a run. One number, recorded once per capture, and every plant dimension becomes metric. This is the least effort for the most immediately useful change, since "how big is this cabbage" is the question the harvestability goal actually needs answered.

6. Drive at a constant speed

The robot moves in bursts, and it makes the footage much harder to use than it needs to be. Keyframes have to be chosen on measured drift rather than at fixed intervals, because some one-second gaps are pixel-identical and others change the frame almost completely. On cam3 the median gap between keyframes is 0.134 s against a mean of 0.49 s — the spread that uneven spacing produces is what broke the rig solve's pose pairing until it was reworked to pair in time rather than by sample index.

A steady crawl would make keyframe selection trivial, give every consecutive pair a comparable triangulation baseline, and remove the stationary stretches that have to be detected and dropped.

7. Resolution, shutter and lighting

640×480 is the floor for this kind of work, and a cabbage canopy is a self-similar repeated texture — the same problem the NSF snap-bean reconstruction work describes. More pixels on the plant directly improves both depth and detection. A global shutter matters because the camera is moving on a vibrating chassis; rolling-shutter skew is a systematic distortion that no amount of downstream solving can remove. Consistent lighting, or capture in overcast conditions, reduces the specular variation that makes matching across frames harder than it should be.

What is worth doing without new footage

Three things could still be improved in software on the existing clips, though none of them lift the ceiling the way overlap would:

  • Keep every track segment. When reconstruction breaks track it currently keeps only the largest segment and discards the rest. Hand-eye constraints are valid within a segment, so the discarded ones could still contribute to the rig solve even though they sit in unrelated coordinate frames. cam1's whole-clip run threw away everything outside a 52-second window.
  • Register on plants instead of pixels. The cameras share no matchable texture, but they do observe the same objects. Aligning cam1's detected plant positions to cam3's as a 2D point-set registration in the ground plane is a correspondence that does not need shared imagery. It is the most promising route to fixing cross-camera matching without touching the rig.
  • Per-camera confidence floors. The ground cameras produce most of the junk — the height trim removes 20.8% of cam2's points and 11.4% of cam1's against 1.9% of cam3's. A stricter depth-confidence threshold for the ground cameras specifically would be more principled than trimming on height after the fact.

If only one thing changes

Angle the ground cameras so they overlap the nadir camera's view. It costs a bracket, it needs no new hardware, and it converts the hardest part of this problem from an inference into a measurement. Everything else on this page is an improvement; that one is a change of kind.
