VLM Baseline

The task

This track asks a simpler question than the reconstruction work: shown a single frame of a crop row, can a vision-language model name the crop and judge its growth stage? Three models were given an identical set of 72 frames and an identical prompt, then scored against each other. The point was to establish what off-the-shelf models already manage before any training is attempted.

Every model ran locally on an Apple M4, which rules out the largest models and makes speed a real criterion rather than an afterthought. Crop type has free ground truth, because each source clip contains exactly one crop and the filename records it. Growth stage has no ground truth at all, which turned out to be the more interesting half of the result.

Libraries used

LibraryWhat it doesWhy it, and not the alternative
MLX and mlx-vlm Runs vision-language models directly on the M4's GPU. Chosen over PyTorch, whose Apple backend is slower for this workload and lacks quantised builds of these particular models. MLX is Apple's own framework and targets this hardware specifically.
Hugging Face Hub Fetches the three model checkpoints and caches them locally. Chosen over vendoring weights by hand because the models are distributed there anyway, and the cache makes repeat runs free.
FFmpeg Samples frames out of the source clips at a fixed interval. Chosen over OpenCV here because this track needs nothing but decoding, and FFmpeg handles the odd MJPEG files without fuss.
Pillow Draws each model's verdict onto the frames to produce review footage. Chosen over matplotlib because the output is a composited image rather than a plot, which is Pillow's job and not matplotlib's.

All three models returned valid, schema-conforming output on every one of the 216 calls, so reliability was never the limitation. Qwen was both the fastest and the most accurate, running seven times quicker than Molmo for better results. On crop identification the task is evidently within reach of a four-billion-parameter model running on a laptop.

Growth stage is where the picture falls apart. There is no ground truth, so the strongest available signal is whether independently trained models agree — and all three agree on the stage for only 2 of 72 frames. Molmo and Qwen agree on stage just 11% of the time, which is close to what random labelling would produce.

What the 3D work revealed

Every model called the pepper clips "tomato", and the manifest's ground truth recorded tomato as well, so Qwen scored a perfect 100%. The overhead camera used by the reconstruction track later settled it: the plants are unmistakably chili peppers. The ground truth was wrong, the models were wrong, and the benchmark quietly rewarded the error.

This is the clearest argument for the reconstruction track. A ground-level camera buried in foliage sees red fruit on a bushy plant and tomato is a reasonable guess, whereas the view from directly above resolves the shape immediately. Perspective was doing more work than model choice.

Next steps

Stage assessment needs labels before it needs better models, because without ground truth there is no way to tell a good answer from a confident one. The frames where the three models disagree are the ones worth labelling first, since they mark exactly where the task is hard. Correcting the pepper mislabel in the manifest is the immediate fix.

Beyond that, the two tracks should converge. A stage judgement is far more useful attached to a specific plant at a known position than to an anonymous frame, which is precisely what the reconstruction produces. Feeding each detected plant's cropped image to a VLM, rather than a whole frame, would give per-plant ripeness on a field map the AMIGA can act on.
